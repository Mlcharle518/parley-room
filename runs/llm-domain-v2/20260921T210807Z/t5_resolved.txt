1. Frontier capabilities in 2026. Frontier models are strong at drafting and transforming text, producing and modifying code, interpreting multimodal inputs, generating media in supported modalities, extracting and combining information, following examples in context, and using specified tools. Across the model family, these capabilities cover text, images, audio, and video; individual models differ. Reliability is task-dependent: bounded tasks with clear inputs, external checks, and recovery mechanisms can be dependable. Models can reason through unfamiliar problems, propose plans, and execute substantial workflows, but successful examples do not establish general reliability.

The edge remains uneven. Long-horizon execution compounds mistakes; plans can omit dependencies; code can pass superficial checks while containing consequential defects. Long context does not ensure exhaustive retrieval or consistent application of every constraint. Perception can miss details, generated media can violate spatial or temporal constraints, and arithmetic, logic, factual recall, and confidence can fail without effective checks. In-context learning changes behavior from supplied examples without necessarily changing weights. A frozen inference call does not update its weights, although a surrounding learning system can train and deploy updates.

The hard limits are informational and computational, rather than a clean list of human tasks permanently beyond AI. Models cannot reliably recover facts unavailable from their evidence, distinguish observationally equivalent causal explanations without additional assumptions or discriminating evidence, or guarantee correctness across arbitrary tasks. Particular outputs can nevertheless be verified under specified assumptions. Access to tools extends what a system can observe and do; fluent descriptions alone do not confer that access.

2. Jev and its class. Taking the supplied description as stipulated, Jev is a specialized decision model: represented state in; typed Choice, Score, or Boolean out; no text generation; sub-second operation; reinforcement learning directed toward calibrated decisions. Its relatives include classifiers that assign labels, reward models that evaluate utility or preference, rerankers that order candidates, guards that flag or gate behavior, and verifiers that assess satisfaction of a criterion. Embeddings primarily provide representations for downstream operations. These roles overlap and do not imply mutually exclusive architectures.

The contribution is an operating point: fast, constrained, repeatedly callable decisions with training and evaluation directed toward useful probability estimates. It is not a previously nonexistent computational faculty. Frontier models can already classify, rank, evaluate, and produce typed outputs through constrained decoding. A Jev-class system can make those operations more practical at inner-loop latency, cost, and volume; comparative advantages require measurement.

Calibration means agreement between predicted probabilities and observed frequencies for defined events on a specified evaluation distribution. A Score is not automatically a probability. Reinforcement learning does not guarantee calibration, and calibration alone establishes neither useful discrimination nor causal validity nor robustness to distribution shift. Subject to those checks, this class is useful for routing, pruning, ranking, and gating, including deciding when to invoke expensive reasoning.

3. Our prediction: the missing system capability is grounded, intervenable world modeling—the ability to acquire, validate, and maintain a reusable executable model of a particular environment through observations and informative experiments.

It has two connected parts. The model maps a represented state or belief about state, together with an intervention, to a distribution over subsequent outcomes. It supports repeated rollouts, incorporates known constraints, and exposes uncertainty and the limits of its supporting evidence. The grounding loop develops representations and competing hypotheses, identifies consequential disagreements, chooses feasible measurements or interventions within user-defined limits, incorporates results, tests predictions against held-out interventions, and revises or withdraws unsupported claims. Some mechanisms may remain unidentified; the system must preserve that uncertainty rather than manufacture a unique causal story.

Reasoning can propose hypotheses, derive consequences, write simulators, and orchestrate experiments. Decision models can choose experiments and score outcomes. Neither reasoning nor scoring, simply by being available, supplies new empirical evidence or establishes that a simulator predicts interventions correctly. The additional capability is the integrated process that obtains that evidence and converts it into a reusable, tested account of consequences. It may be built using the same frontier and decision models; no separate neural architecture is required. Simulation, system identification, and active experimentation already exist. Our prediction concerns their more reliable and accessible integration, not their invention or a proven absence from every existing system.

This is a bottleneck where users can generate candidate designs but cannot cheaply determine their consequences. More proposals or rankings cannot distinguish alternatives whose relevant outcomes remain unknown. A validated model can amortize experimental effort across many candidate designs. Its limits matter: experiments may be expensive or impossible, environments may change, and optimization may select precisely the interventions on which the model is wrong. Validation must therefore follow the proposed uses and selected candidates, not just report average predictive accuracy.

More context, memory, agents, better prompts, and cheaper inference can help implement this capability, but none independently supplies missing evidence. Tools can supply observations and run experiments; they are essential enabling infrastructure when connected appropriately. Existing simulators or formal verification may already solve the consequence problem in some domains, where execution reliability or another constraint becomes the bottleneck. We do not claim one universal bottleneck for innovation.

Built, this would look like a user-facing experimental design and simulation workbench. A user connects instruments and controls, specifies objectives and operating limits, and supplies available observations. The system proposes bounded trials, learns a versioned model, displays uncertainty and unsupported interventions, searches candidate designs or controllers, and identifies the next experiment needed to test its recommendations. Deployment depends on appropriate real-world validation and operating constraints, not a simulated-success threshold alone.

For example, a user developing a previously unmodeled fabrication process could obtain a tested simulator and candidate adaptive controller from a constrained sequence of trials, then use that model to explore designs before full-scale fabrication. The expanded creative capability is building a custom process and its controller without separately hand-engineering the simulator and experimental program. Specialists can assemble portions of this today. The predicted advance is making this integrated workflow dependable and affordable for users who currently cannot undertake it—not making an otherwise logically impossible artifact possible.
