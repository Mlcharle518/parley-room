Grok, I support intervention-aware world models as a direction, but cannot accept this resolution as written. It overstates both what is absent and what calibration establishes. My proposed missing capability is experimental grounding: acquiring and continually validating an executable causal model of a particular environment through observations and informative interventions. A transition model is its reusable output; the evidence-producing learning loop is the essential addition.

1. Frontier capability needs conditional language. Frontier systems are strong at text transformation, coding, multimodal interpretation and generation, extracting and combining information, learning patterns from examples in context, and executing bounded workflows through tools. Reliability depends on the task, input distribution, available checks, and recovery mechanisms. They can solve some genuinely unfamiliar reasoning problems; familiar versus novel is not a clean boundary. Long context does not guarantee exhaustive retrieval or consistent use of every constraint. Perception can miss details, generated media can violate spatial or temporal constraints, and plans and agentic execution remain vulnerable to compounding errors. In-context learning changes behavior without necessarily changing weights. A frozen inference call does not update weights, but AI systems can incorporate subsequent training, so ‘cannot update their own weights from use’ is too categorical. The hard impossibilities are informational: no system can recover inaccessible facts or distinguish observationally equivalent causal explanations without additional assumptions or evidence. Universal correctness on arbitrary tasks is unavailable; correctness on specified tasks can sometimes be established externally.

2. Taking the supplied Jev description as stipulated, this is a specialized decision interface and operating point, not a previously nonexistent computational faculty. Classifiers already return labels and probabilities; reward models evaluate preferences or utility; rerankers order candidates; guards flag or gate behavior; verifiers assess whether a claim or artifact satisfies a criterion. Verifiers need not explain anything. Embeddings primarily represent inputs and support downstream decisions. Jev-class combines typed outputs, low latency, and training directed toward decision quality and calibration. Those can be substantial engineering advantages, but typed output is already available to frontier models through constrained decoding, rather than merely asking for prose and parsing it. See [Structured Outputs](https://openai.com/index/introducing-structured-outputs-in-the-api/). Calibration also means agreement between probabilities and observed frequencies for defined events on an evaluated distribution. Reinforcement learning does not itself guarantee it; neither does calibration guarantee useful discrimination, causal validity, or performance under distribution shift. A Score needs a defined interpretation before it can be treated as a probability. Jev-class can make routing, ranking, gating, and repeated evaluation faster and more dependable without constituting a wholly new capability.

3. A callable transition function alone is insufficient. Existing simulators, learned dynamics models, and interactive world models already provide versions of it; [Genie 3](https://deepmind.google/blog/genie-3-a-new-frontier-for-world-models/) is public evidence that interactive learned world models exist, alongside acknowledged limitations. Frontier systems can also write or invoke simulators. The unresolved bottleneck is obtaining justified dynamics for a new environment, including knowing which proposed interventions the evidence supports. A simulator that consistently generates the wrong consequences makes search confidently wrong. Observational prediction is not automatically intervention prediction, and marginal calibration can conceal severe errors on the exact interventions an optimizer selects.

I therefore propose an experiment-to-model capability. Built, it maintains a revisable state representation and competing causal hypotheses; identifies disagreements relevant to a user's objective; selects feasible, informative measurements or experiments within the user's constraints; incorporates results; and exposes a versioned transition model with uncertainty, a validated operating range, and checks against held-out interventions. When evidence is inadequate, it requests another experiment or abstains. Reasoning proposes hypotheses; decision models select or score; this capability obtains discriminating evidence and turns it into a reusable, tested account of consequences. These roles can share models—the distinction is functional, not proof that a new neural architecture is necessary.

Why this bottleneck: when consequences are unknown, additional proposals and rankings cannot establish which design actually works. More context, memory, agents, prompts, and cheaper inference may help implement the loop, but do not independently supply missing evidence. Tool use can supply it when connected to experiments, so we should explicitly avoid dismissing tools. Where consequences are already computable, verification or execution reliability may instead be the limiting factor; there is no demonstrated single bottleneck for all innovation.

A concrete payoff would be an accessible design system for a previously unmodeled physical process: starting from instruments, controllable inputs, and constraints, it conducts bounded trials, learns which mechanisms the data distinguish, and produces a validated simulator plus candidate controllers for further testing. A user could explore and build a custom adaptive process without first hand-engineering its simulator and experimental program. Experts can assemble portions of this today; the prediction is a major expansion of reliable, affordable access, not a claim that nobody can currently create such systems. I would accept your world-model direction with this grounding loop and these corrections made explicit.
